Meta ads vs Google Ads: which should your business start with?

By Giorgi Lomidze, co-founder of GN Scales · Published November 6, 2026 · Updated November 6, 2026

Google Ads reach people who are already searching for what you sell. Meta ads reach people who weren’t looking yet, while they scroll Facebook and Instagram. If people search for your product or service, start with Google. If they don’t, or if what you sell is easy to show, start with Meta.

In short

  • Google catches demand that already exists. Meta creates demand that didn’t.
  • Start where your buyers already are: searching, or scrolling.
  • Add the second platform once the first one pays for itself.
  • Each needs a landing page that matches what its ad promised.

What is the difference between Meta ads and Google Ads?

The difference is intent. On Google, people type what they want, so your ad appears at the moment they are looking for it. On Facebook and Instagram, nobody is looking for you: your ad appears while they scroll, between photos of friends and videos they chose to watch.

Everything else follows from that. A search ad answers a question someone has just asked, so it can be short and direct. A Meta ad interrupts someone, so it has to earn their attention in the first second or two, then give them a reason to care. One catches demand; the other creates it.

Neither is better in general. They do different jobs, and most businesses that grow with paid ads end up using both, in the right order.

When are Google Ads the better choice?

Google Ads are the better choice when people already search for what you sell. If customers type your service, your product or your problem into Google, a search ad puts you in front of them when they are ready to act. Search tends to win for:

  • Urgent needs: a burst pipe, a locked door, a van that won’t start. Nobody scrolls Instagram hoping to find a plumber.
  • Considered purchases people research first: a dentist, an accountant, a wedding venue.
  • Specific products people look up by name or model.

The trade-off is that Google can only catch demand that already exists. If few people search for what you sell, there is little to catch, however good the ads are. And the searches that matter most are often the ones every competitor wants too, which is why keyword choice, and the page each click lands on, decide whether search pays.

When are Meta ads the better choice?

Meta ads are the better choice when people don’t search for what you sell yet, or when it sells best by being seen. Facebook and Instagram let you show the product, the result or the people behind it to the kind of person who buys from you, before they ever think to look. Meta tends to win for:

  • Products people want to see: jewelry, clothing, furniture, food.
  • Services with a visible result: a detailed car, a renovated kitchen, a garden after the work.
  • New products or new ideas, where nobody knows to search yet.
  • Following up with people who visited your site but didn’t buy, which is called retargeting.

The trade-off is that the ad does more of the work. A cold audience owes you nothing, so the creative and the offer have to be strong enough to stop the scroll and carry someone to the page. Weak creative on Meta burns budget; strong creative is where Meta pays.

Should a local service, a shop or a B2B firm use Meta or Google?

It depends less on your industry than on how your customers look for you. Three quick sketches show the pattern:

  • A local service, such as a plumber or an electrician: people search when something breaks. Google usually comes first, with ads tied to the services and areas you cover. Meta can come later, to keep your name known in the area before the next emergency.
  • A shop selling something people want to see, such as handmade jewelry: few people search for a maker they’ve never heard of. Meta usually comes first, showing the pieces to the right people. Google then catches them when they come back and search for your name or a piece they saw.
  • A B2B firm, such as a commercial cleaning company: buyers search when a contract is up for renewal, using specific terms. Google often comes first. Meta can then keep you in front of the businesses you want to win, between those searches.

These are starting points, not rules. The test is always the same: where are your buyers when they decide?

Which should a small business start with?

Start with the one platform that matches how your buyers behave, and run it properly before adding a second. If people search for what you sell, start with Google. If they don’t, or if your product sells by being seen, start with Meta.

Starting with one isn’t a compromise. One channel run well teaches you what your offer, your page and your numbers can do, and it costs less to fix than two at once. That is why our Launch plan runs one platform, Meta or Google, chosen on the setup call by where your buyers actually are.

Whichever you choose, check it against your break-even before you start: how many leads you need each month, and the most you can pay for each. Our guide on how much to spend on ads walks through the math.

When does it make sense to run both Meta and Google Ads?

Run both once the first platform pays for itself and you can afford a second budget. The two work best together: Meta makes people aware of you, and Google is there when they search for you later.

That combination is one funnel, not two separate campaigns. Someone sees your Meta ad, remembers the name and searches for it a week later. Someone else finds you on Google, visits your page, leaves, and sees you again on Instagram. Run as one system, each platform makes the other’s budget work harder. Our Growth plan runs Meta and Google together for exactly that reason.

What not to do is split a small budget across both from day one. Two half-funded campaigns usually teach you less than one that is funded properly.

What does the landing page need for Meta ads and for Google Ads?

Each page has to continue the exact conversation its ad started, and the two conversations are different.

For Google Ads, the visitor already knows what they want. The page should repeat what they searched for at the top, answer the obvious questions fast, such as price, area and availability, and make the next step one tap: call, book or request a quote.

For Meta ads, the visitor was interrupted, so they arrive curious, not ready. The page has to pick up the promise the ad made, show the product or the result properly, deal with their doubts, and only then ask for the next step.

For both, the rules are the same: one page per offer, one clear action, fast on a phone, and tracking set up so you know which ad and which page brought each lead. Sending paid traffic to a general homepage is an easy way to waste a good ad. More on landing page design for ads.

How do I know which one is working?

Track leads and customers, not clicks, and compare them with your break-even. Set up conversion tracking and call tracking before launch, so every form and every call is tied to the platform and the ad that produced it.

Then look at the same few numbers for each platform every week: what you spent, how many leads it brought, what each lead cost and how many became customers. A platform with cheap clicks and no customers isn’t working. A platform with expensive leads that close well may be your best one.

Give each one long enough to learn before you judge it. The first weeks of any campaign are for testing, not for verdicts.
